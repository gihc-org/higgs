Given you've already got a Raspberry Pi, a laptop, and Tailscale wired together for your k3s setup, the cheapest real IPFS cluster is one that just adds a second/third peer to hardware you already own — you don't need to pay for anything if you're okay with modest storage/bandwidth.

**Core setup**

1. **Run Kubo (go-ipfs) on 2-3 nodes** you control — laptop + Pi is enough for a real cluster, not a single point of failure. Add a cheap VPS (~$4-5/mo, e.g. Hetzner CX22 or a low-tier Contabo box) only if you want a publicly reachable gateway/bootstrap node that's up 24/7 (your laptop won't always be).
2. **Add ipfs-cluster-service** on each node, orchestrating pinning across peers. For 2-3 nodes, use **CRDT consensus** (not Raft) — it's lighter, doesn't need a fixed quorum, and tolerates nodes going offline/online (which matters since your laptop isn't always up).
3. **Tie it to Tailscale** — since you already have Tailscale between your Pi and phone, extend that overlay network to the cluster peers so they gossip over a private network instead of exposing cluster ports publicly.

**Cheapest-possible topology for you specifically**
- Pi = always-on peer + pinner (this becomes your "reliable" node)
- Laptop = second peer, contributes storage/bandwidth when on
- Optional: one cheap VPS as a third peer purely for public gateway access (`/ipfs/<cid>` reachable from the internet) — this is the only cash cost, and you can skip it entirely if you only need cluster access over Tailscale

**On your k3s angle:** since you're mid-migration to k3s, you could run Kubo + ipfs-cluster-service as pods (there are community Helm charts, or just plain Deployments/StatefulSets with PVCs), letting k3s handle restarts/scheduling instead of your systemd-user-service + `loginctl enable-linger` approach. That's arguably cleaner than what you were doing for IPFS automation, though the systemd route works fine on the Pi if you don't want IPFS in the cluster's blast radius.

**What to skip to keep it free**
- Skip Pinata/Filecoin/Lighthouse entirely for now — you already hit the paid-tier pin-by-CID wall there. Self-pinning on your own peers avoids that completely; you lose "guaranteed geographic redundancy" but for a portfolio project (ipfs-apps) that's not a real requirement yet.
- Skip Raft consensus — it wants an odd number of always-on peers for quorum, which fights your "laptop sleeps sometimes" reality.

If you want, I can sketch the actual `ipfs-cluster-service init` + systemd unit files, or a k3s manifest for running this as pods instead.

That changes the calculus a fair bit — a homelab-only cluster is fine for a portfolio piece, but for a public Atom feed serving media to real listeners/podcast apps, "reliable and always reachable" starts to matter more than "cheap and clever."

A few things worth thinking through before locking in an architecture:

**The reachability problem**
Podcast/feed clients fetch media over plain HTTPS — they don't speak IPFS natively. So your actual requirement isn't "an IPFS cluster," it's "a domain + gateway that's always up, backed by IPFS storage." That gateway is the single point that can't go down, which matters because your laptop sleeping is a "meh" problem for a portfolio pin but an "episode won't download" problem for a real feed.

**Cheapest way to get that reliably:**
- Keep your Pi and laptop as pinning peers (free, using hardware you have) — this is your redundant storage layer via CRDT-consensus ipfs-cluster, as before.
- But route the *public-facing gateway* through something always-on. Since you're already building ingress-nginx + cert-manager into your k3s migration, the natural move is: run a Kubo gateway pod in-cluster, expose it via your existing ingress, and DNSLink your feed's media URLs to it. If your k3s runs on the Pi/laptop themselves, you're back to the "not always on" problem — worth checking where your k3s nodes actually live before this is free.
- If none of your always-on hardware can host the gateway, a ~$4-5/mo VPS as one cluster peer + the public gateway is the one place I'd spend money — everything else (Pi, laptop) stays free pinning capacity.

**A cost trap to watch:** audio files are much heavier than the static frontend assets your ipfs-apps project serves. If listener numbers grow, you'll be serving real bandwidth from whatever box hosts the gateway — home upload bandwidth or a budget VPS's bandwidth cap could become the actual constraint, not storage.

**One thing that'd help me tighten this up:** roughly how many listeners/how much traffic are you expecting, and do you know yet whether your k3s nodes will live on the Pi/laptop or somewhere with better uptime? That determines whether you need to pay for anything at all or can do this entirely on hardware you own.

### 🤖 Claude

That reframes it well — the core value you're after isn't "IPFS specifically," it's *not being one company's decision away from your feed disappearing*. That's a legitimate design goal, and it splits into a few independent layers that don't all need to be solved with the same tool:

**Storage/distribution layer** — this is what IPFS is genuinely good for. Kubo + ipfs-cluster pinning across your Hetzner VPS, Pi, and laptop gives you actual protocol-level redundancy: the media exists as content-addressed data with no single host being "the" source of truth. This part of your plan is solid as-is.

**Addressing layer (how a feed URL points at "current" content)** — this is where vendor-neutrality gets more nuanced:
- **IPNS** (IPFS's own naming system) is the "purest" option — no DNS registrar involved at all, just a self-owned key. Downside: resolution is slow and somewhat flaky in practice, which matters for a feed real listeners hit repeatedly.
- **DNSLink** is a pragmatic middle ground — you still use a normal domain, but the domain only points at *content hashes*, not at any specific CDN or host. Losing your registrar is a much smaller, much rarer failure than losing a CDN vendor, so this mostly satisfies "not tied to a provider" without IPNS's reliability cost.
- **Handshake** replaces the registrar itself, which is philosophically the most complete answer — but near-zero client/browser support today means almost nobody can resolve it without a special resolver or proxy. Good research territory, not viable as your feed's only address yet.

**WebTorrent** is a genuinely different value proposition from IPFS: swarm-based delivery driven by whoever's actively seeding, vs. IPFS's content-addressed pinning model. For a media feed, I'd frame it less as "instead of IPFS" and more as a second, independent distribution path — a feed item could carry both an IPFS CID and a magnet link, so no single protocol's health determines availability. That's arguably a stronger "not tied to any provider" story than either alone, and it's a nice research angle (multi-protocol redundancy).

**Given this is portfolio/research first:** I'd sequence it rather than build all three at once —
1. Ship the real thing first: IPFS storage + cluster across your three boxes, DNSLink for the feed URL. This alone already proves the "no vendor CDN" thesis and gets you a working feed.
2. Layer in IPNS as an alternative address once DNSLink is stable, so you can compare/demonstrate both.
3. Treat WebTorrent and Handshake as explicit "phase 2" experiments — document them as such in the project, since bundling everything up front is the likeliest way this stalls before shipping anything.
